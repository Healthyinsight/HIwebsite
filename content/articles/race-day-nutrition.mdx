You're standing on the start line feeling perfectly tapered and ready. The first half of the race feels effortless, but suddenly, at mile 18, it feels like you're running through wet concrete. You didn't undertrain. You under-fueled. The dreaded "wall" isn't a fitness problem. It's a math problem.

## Here's the thing

Optimal race day nutrition isn't something you can wing on the course. Research shows that endurance athletes consistently overestimate how many carbohydrates they consume during a race, often falling 16-17% short of their actual fueling plans. To perform at your peak, your fueling strategy must be mathematically precise and practiced long before the starting gun fires.

## Here's what to do: 5 steps

1. **The carb load (36-48 hours out):** Reduce your training volume to zero and consume 10-12 grams of carbohydrates per kilogram of body weight per day. This maximizes your muscle glycogen stores.
2. **The pre-race breakfast (2-4 hours prior):** Eat at least 100 grams of easily digestible carbohydrates (e.g., white toast, jam, oatmeal). Avoid high-fiber and high-fat foods, which slow down digestion.
3. **During the race (1-2.5 hours):** Aim for a steady intake of 30-60 grams of carbohydrates per hour.
4. **During the race (over 2.5 hours):** Increase your intake to 60-90 grams per hour. To absorb this much without GI distress, use products containing a mix of glucose and fructose (multiple transportable carbohydrates).
5. **Set an alarm:** Don't eat "when you feel hungry." Fuel based on a strict time or distance schedule (e.g., one gel every 30 minutes).

## What the research shows

Contemporary sports nutrition guidelines clearly establish the dose-response relationship between carbohydrate intake and endurance performance. A comprehensive 2019 review confirmed that consuming 60-90 g/h of dual-source carbohydrates is optimal for long-distance events to prevent glycogen depletion.[1](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/29/2/article-p117.xml) However, knowing the guidelines isn't enough. A 2023 observational study found that marathoners and cyclists consistently overestimate their mid-race fueling, under-consuming their planned carbohydrate intake by an average of 16-17%.[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12501108/)

## Why it works

Your body has two primary fuel tanks: fat and glycogen (stored carbohydrates). Fat is a virtually limitless tank, but it burns slowly and requires a lot of oxygen. Glycogen is high-octane rocket fuel, but the tank is extremely small.

Even fully carb-loaded, your muscles only store enough glycogen for about 90 to 120 minutes of hard running. Once that tank is empty, your body is forced to switch entirely to fat oxidation. Because fat takes longer to convert to usable energy, your pace drops drastically, which is what we call "hitting the wall." By consuming exogenous (external) carbohydrates during the race, you continuously top off the rocket fuel tank, delaying the point of depletion and allowing you to maintain your goal pace all the way to the finish line.

## Avoid these mistakes

- **Fiber loading the night before:** A big plate of whole-wheat pasta and broccoli the night before a race is a recipe for severe GI distress. Switch to low-fiber, "white" carbs 24 hours out.
- **Trying new fuel on race day:** If you haven't used a specific brand of gel or sports drink during your long training runs, do not touch it on the race course.
- **Winging the math:** Don't grab a gel just because you see an aid station. Calculate exactly how many grams of carbs are in your products, and write down exactly what mile or kilometer you need to consume them.
